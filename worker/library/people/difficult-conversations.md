Every project has conversations a PM would rather postpone: telling a sponsor the date will slip, holding a vendor to a commitment they are missing, raising a performance problem with someone on the team. Postponing them almost always makes them harder. The aim is not to win, but to leave the room with a shared picture of the facts and an agreed next step.

## Before: prepare on paper

Spend fifteen minutes writing down:

- **Your goal.** What should be true when the conversation ends? "The vendor commits to a recovery plan with dates by Friday" is a goal. "The vendor understands how upset we are" is not.
- **The facts.** Dates, numbers, what was promised and what happened, with sources. Things a camera would have recorded.
- **Your story.** What you have concluded from the facts: "they do not take our account seriously". Label it as a story; it may be wrong.
- **Their likely view.** What pressures are they under? What would they say is the cause?
- **What you can offer and what you cannot.** Know your limits before you start.
- **Your opening sentence**, written out.

Choose the setting: in person or on video for anything serious, privately, with enough time. Never in a steering committee in front of others, and never by chat.

## Opening: say it plainly, early

Name the topic in the first minute, without warming up for ten. A useful shape is: the subject, the facts, why it matters, and an invitation.

> "I want to talk about the wave 2 installs. We planned twelve sites by the 30th and we have five. That puts the customer's go-live at risk. I'd like to understand what's happening on your side and agree a plan."

Avoid opening with your story ("you've been unreliable"). It invites a defence of character instead of a discussion of the problem.

## Listening

Then stop talking. Ask open questions: "What's getting in the way?", "What would you need to make the 15th?". Summarise what you heard before answering: "So the crews were moved to another account and the replacement team needs training. Did I get that right?". People who feel heard are far more willing to commit to a fix. Silence is useful; let it run.

## Separating facts from stories

Both sides arrive with stories. When the conversation heats up, go back to what can be checked: "Let's look at the dates together." Offer your story as a hypothesis, not a verdict: "From where I sit, it looks like our sites lost priority. Is that fair?". Invite theirs the same way. Often the story turns out to be half right, and the fixable half becomes visible.

## Closing: an agreed next step

Do not end on "let's keep an eye on it". End with one specific step, an owner and a date: "You'll send a revised install schedule by Thursday noon with crew names; I'll brief the customer on Friday with it." Summarise it out loud, then confirm it in writing the same day. If you could not agree, say so and name the next step anyway, including escalation if needed (see [[issues-and-escalation]]).

## Three common cases

**With a sponsor (bad news).** Bring it early, with the facts, the impact and options with trade-offs, not just the problem. Make a recommendation. Ask for the decision you need. Sponsors rarely punish early bad news; they remember being surprised.

**With a vendor (missed commitments).** Anchor on the contract and the agreed schedule, but look for the cause before the remedy. Ask for a recovery plan with dates, and agree how progress will be checked. Keep the relationship intact; you will need them next month (see [[vendor-management]]).

**With a team member (performance or behaviour).** Do it privately and soon. Describe the specific behaviour and its effect on the project, not the person's character. Ask for their view; there may be a workload or personal reason you do not know. Agree what changes and when you will check in. If they report to someone else, involve their manager properly rather than going around them.

## In Compass

On a project that allows cloud AI, Athena's "Prepare a difficult conversation" preset drafts a goal, an opening line, facts versus stories, likely reactions and the next step to agree. Treat it as preparation, not a script. Log the agreed next step as a task with its date, and any decision in Decisions.

## In practice

- Name the conversation you have been postponing and book it within five working days.
- Write your goal, facts and story on one page before you go in.
- Draft your opening sentence and say it aloud once.
- After the conversation, send a written summary of the agreed step the same day.
- Add a task to check the agreed step on its due date.
