title: Stakeholder management

Projects rarely fail because nobody knew what to build. They fail more often because someone with influence was surprised, ignored or quietly opposed. Stakeholder management (gestion des parties prenantes) is the deliberate work of finding those people early, understanding what each one needs from the project, and spending your limited time where it changes the outcome.

## Who counts as a stakeholder

Anyone who can affect the project or will feel its effects. On a B2B telecom deal that list is longer than it first looks:

- **Customer side:** the executive who signed, the IT director who must live with the service, the network team that will run it, procurement, site contacts who hold the keys, security and compliance.
- **Bell side:** the sponsor, the account team who owns the relationship, sales engineering who designed the solution, operations and the assurance teams who take over, finance, legal for contract questions.
- **Third parties:** carriers, equipment vendors, installers, building landlords, sometimes a regulator.

Ways to find the ones you missed: walk the WBS and ask who touches each deliverable; read the contract for named approvers; ask each stakeholder "who else should I be talking to?"; and look at who signed off the last similar project.

## The power / interest grid

Place each stakeholder on two axes: how much they can influence the project (power) and how much they care about it (interest). The four boxes suggest a default approach:

| | Low interest | High interest |
|---|---|---|
| **High power** | Keep satisfied: short, well-timed updates; no surprises | Manage closely: involve in decisions, meet regularly |
| **Low power** | Monitor: watch for changes, light touch | Keep informed: regular, useful information; listen |

The grid is a starting point, not a verdict. People move: a customer's procurement lead with little interest during design becomes very interested when an invoice disputes the contract. A quiet site manager can stop a cutover by refusing access. Revisit the grid at each phase.

## Engagement levels

The grid tells you where someone is; engagement tells you where you need them to be. A common five-step scale:

- **Unaware** of the project or its effect on them.
- **Resistant**: aware and opposed.
- **Neutral**: aware, neither for nor against.
- **Supportive**: aware and in favour.
- **Leading**: actively pushing the project forward.

For each key stakeholder, note the current level and the level you need. The gap is the work. You do not need everyone leading; you need the customer's IT director at least supportive before cutover and the operations manager at least neutral before handover. When someone is resistant, find out what they stand to lose. Often it is workload, control or credibility, and the fix is involvement rather than persuasion.

## The stakeholder register

Keep one table (registre des parties prenantes). Useful columns:

| Column | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Name and role | Including organisation |
| What they care about | Their interest in their own words, not yours |
| Power and interest | High or low on each, or a 1–5 score |
| Current engagement | Unaware to leading |
| Needed engagement | And by when |
| Approach | How you will close the gap |
| Owner | Who on the team holds the relationship |
| Channel and language | Preferred format, frequency, English or French |
| Last contact | Date, so neglected people stand out |

Keep it private. A register that says "resistant, protects his team's budget" should never end up in a shared folder with the customer.

## In Compass

There is no separate stakeholder table: keep the register as a document in the project, record the sponsor on the project record, and log stakeholder risks (a key approver leaving, a resistant operations team) in the RAID log, where they get a score and an owner. On a work project, Compass encrypts both the document and the RAID rows at rest.

## In practice

- List ten stakeholders on your current project and place them on the grid; look hardest at the high-power, low-interest box.
- For the three with the biggest engagement gap, write one concrete action each for this week.
- Ask your sponsor and the account manager who they think you have missed.
- Add a "last contact" date to every row and call the oldest one.
- Log the stakeholder risk you worry about most in the RAID log with an owner.
