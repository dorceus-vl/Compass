title: PMBOK 6: the ten knowledge areas
section: Frameworks
---
In the sixth edition of the PMBOK Guide, each of the 49 processes belongs to one of ten knowledge areas: a subject a project manager has to handle, whatever stage the project is in. Read across with the five process groups, they form the familiar grid of processes.

## Integration management

The area that ties the others together. It covers the charter, the overall project management plan, directing the work, managing project knowledge, integrated change control and closing. When a vendor delay hits schedule, cost and the customer's go-live at once, integration is where you weigh the trade-offs and decide.

## Scope management

Defining and controlling what is and is not included. It runs from collecting requirements to the work breakdown structure (WBS) and the customer's acceptance of deliverables. On an enterprise install, scope says which sites, which services and which demarcation point; "out of scope" matters as much as "in scope" (portée).

## Schedule management

Turning the work into activities, sequencing them, estimating durations and building a schedule you can control. Telecom schedules are dominated by external lead times: building access, permits, carrier handoffs and equipment delivery. Know your critical path and which dates you do not control.

## Cost management

Estimating, budgeting and controlling cost against a baseline. Earned value lives here. On B2B work, watch the gap between what the customer pays and the cost to deliver, including truck rolls, vendor professional services and rework.

## Quality management

Planning what quality means, assuring that processes will deliver it and controlling the results. In practice: acceptance criteria per service, test plans for turn-up, and checking that the last installs did not fail for the same reason.

## Resource management

Planning, acquiring and leading people and physical resources. You rarely own the people on a telecom project; field crews, engineers and vendor staff are shared. Agree commitments with their managers in advance and confirm them before each window.

## Communications management

Getting the right information to the right people at the right time and in the right form. A communications plan names audiences, content, frequency and channel: weekly status to the customer, daily stand-up with the field team, monthly report to the steering committee (comité directeur).

## Risk management

Identifying, analysing, responding to and monitoring risks, threats and opportunities alike. The risk register and its regular review sit here. Typical telecom risks: late equipment, failed site readiness, a customer change freeze, a dependency on another carrier.

## Procurement management

Buying goods and services from outside: choosing the contract type, selecting sellers, controlling delivery and closing contracts. Vendor statements of work, purchase orders and acceptance of vendor deliverables live here.

## Stakeholder management

Identifying stakeholders, planning how to engage them and managing that engagement. It became a separate area in the fifth edition, a sign that engagement is active work, not a mailing list.

## How 6 and 7 relate

The sixth edition is process-based: it tells you which processes exist, with inputs, tools and techniques, and outputs. The seventh edition (2021) is principle-based: twelve principles and eight performance domains describe outcomes and behaviours, and leave the choice of process to you. Seven does not make six wrong. The knowledge areas map loosely onto the domains (risk to Uncertainty, stakeholders to Stakeholders, schedule and cost to Planning and Measurement), and the processes in six remain a useful toolbox when you need a concrete technique. A practical rule: use seven to decide what matters on this project, and six to find a method for doing it.

## In practice

- Walk the ten areas against your project and mark any with no owner or no artefact.
- Check your WBS reaches work packages (lots de travail) small enough to assign and track.
- Write down the three external lead times that drive your schedule and who you chase for each.
- Confirm your communications plan says who gets what, how often, and in which language.
- Review open vendor deliverables and their acceptance criteria.
