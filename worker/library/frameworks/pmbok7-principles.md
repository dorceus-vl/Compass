The seventh edition of PMI's PMBOK Guide (2021) moved away from a catalogue of processes and put twelve principles at its centre. They describe how a good project manager behaves, whatever the method, so they work for a predictive network build and an agile portal alike. PMI has since published a newer edition that reorganises this material; check which edition your PMO and your certification refer to before quoting one.

## The twelve principles

**1. Stewardship.** Look after the money, people and reputation you have been trusted with, and act with integrity even when nobody is checking. Your duty reaches past the project to the customer and the company. *Example:* you decline to mark an enterprise circuit as in service for billing before the customer has tested it, even though it would help the month-end numbers.

**2. Team.** Build a team that shares goals, agrees how it works and feels safe raising problems early. *Example:* provisioning, field technicians and the vendor's engineers agree in writing who calls whom when a turn-up fails during a night window.

**3. Stakeholders.** Find stakeholders early and keep engaging them, because their interest and influence change as the work moves. *Example:* the customer's security lead, ignored at kickoff, blocks a firewall change two days before cutover; a stakeholder map would have surfaced them.

**4. Value.** Judge the project by the outcomes it enables, not only by the deliverables it hands over, and recheck that value as things change. *Example:* on a multi-site SD-WAN migration, you sequence sites by contract renewal risk rather than by geography, because retention is the real benefit.

**5. Systems thinking.** A project is a set of moving parts inside larger systems, so a change in one place ripples elsewhere. *Example:* moving a cutover date touches the customer's change freeze, network operations centre staffing, the vendor's truck rolls and the billing start date.

**6. Leadership.** Leadership is a behaviour anyone on the team can show: setting direction, influencing without authority and adapting your style to the person. *Example:* with a vendor PM who keeps missing dates, you start with a frank one-to-one and a recovery plan before escalating.

**7. Tailoring.** Fit the approach, governance and paperwork to the size, risk and culture of the work; use just enough process. *Example:* a three-site fibre install gets a one-page plan and a weekly call, not the full artefact set of a national migration.

**8. Quality.** Build quality into the way work is done instead of inspecting it at the end, and define what "acceptable" means with the customer. *Example:* a site-readiness checklist (power, space, building access, demarcation point) completed before dispatch cuts failed installs.

**9. Complexity.** Complexity comes from people, interacting systems, ambiguity and uncertainty; watch for it and respond with shorter feedback loops. *Example:* a unified-communications rollout across several vendors starts with one pilot site to learn how the pieces really behave.

**10. Risk.** Manage threats and opportunities with responses that are proportionate, owned and timely. *Example:* you order long-lead optical equipment before final design sign-off because the lead time threat outweighs the small cost of a change.

**11. Adaptability and resiliency.** Design the project so it can absorb shocks and recover quickly. *Example:* every cutover has a tested rollback plan and a second installation crew on standby.

**12. Change.** Projects change how people work, so prepare them to adopt the new state, not just receive it. *Example:* a new customer portal ships with admin training for the customer and a short guide for their end users.

## In practice

- Pick the two principles your current project is weakest on and write one action for each in the project's tasks.
- Draw a quick stakeholder map (parties prenantes) and check every name has an owner on your side.
- For your next cutover, confirm the rollback plan has actually been rehearsed.
- Restate the project's value in one sentence and check the next milestone still serves it.
- Ask the copilot to review your plan against these principles and list the gaps.
