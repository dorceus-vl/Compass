Scrum is a lightweight framework for building a product in short, fixed-length cycles called sprints. It is defined in the Scrum Guide (latest revision 2020), which is deliberately brief: it sets out three accountabilities, five events, three artefacts and a commitment for each artefact, and leaves the practices to the team. In B2B telecom it suits software and digital work, such as a customer portal, an ordering API or network automation, far better than physical builds.

## Accountabilities

A Scrum team is small, typically ten people or fewer, with no sub-teams and no hierarchy inside it.

- **Product Owner.** One person who decides what the team builds next and why. They own the product backlog, its order and its goal. A committee cannot do this job; if three managers can reprioritise, nobody owns the backlog.
- **Scrum Master.** Helps the team and the organisation use Scrum well: coaches, removes impediments, keeps events useful. Not a project manager and not a secretary, though a PM can grow into the role.
- **Developers.** Everyone who does the work of building the increment: developers, testers, designers, analysts. They own the sprint plan and the quality of what they deliver.

## Events

Every event is time-boxed. The limits below are maximums for a one-month sprint; shorter sprints usually use shorter events.

- **The Sprint.** A fixed cycle of one month or less, usually two weeks, containing all the other events. A new sprint starts as soon as the last one ends.
- **Sprint Planning** (up to eight hours). The team agrees why the sprint matters (the sprint goal), which backlog items to take, and how it will deliver them.
- **Daily Scrum** (15 minutes). The developers check progress toward the sprint goal and adjust the day's plan. It is not a status report to a manager.
- **Sprint Review** (up to four hours). The team shows what it built to stakeholders and they discuss what to do next. A working session, not a slide deck.
- **Sprint Retrospective** (up to three hours). The team looks at how it worked and picks improvements for the next sprint.

## Artefacts and their commitments

- **Product Backlog** (carnet de produit), committed to the **Product Goal**: an ordered list of everything the product might need, aimed at a longer-term goal.
- **Sprint Backlog**, committed to the **Sprint Goal**: the items chosen for this sprint plus the plan to deliver them, owned by the developers.
- **Increment**, committed to the **Definition of Done**: each piece of finished, usable work. An item that does not meet the definition of done is not part of the increment and is not shown as done.

The Scrum values (commitment, focus, openness, respect and courage) underpin all of this; when trust is low the events turn into theatre.

## Common failure modes

- **Proxy product owner.** The real decision-maker sits elsewhere and the named product owner relays messages. Priorities flip mid-sprint.
- **Daily status meeting.** The daily scrum becomes a report to the PM, runs 45 minutes and nobody adjusts anything.
- **No definition of done.** "Done" means coded but not tested, or tested but not deployed. Velocity looks good while defects pile up.
- **Sprints with no goal.** The sprint is just a two-week bucket of unrelated tickets; the review has nothing coherent to show.
- **Skipped retrospectives.** The first event dropped when the team is busy, which is exactly when it matters most.
- **Scrum for physical work.** Forcing equipment installs and fibre builds into sprints creates fake increments. Use a predictive plan or Kanban there and keep Scrum for the software.
- **Fixed scope, fixed date, fixed team, "but agile".** If nothing can flex, the framework cannot help; be honest that the delivery is predictive.

## Scrum and the PM

On a hybrid telecom project the PM often sits outside the Scrum team: managing the business case, the customer, the vendors and the gates, while the product owner steers the backlog. The sprint review is your best source of real progress; attend it and bring the customer when you can.

## In practice

- Ask who the single product owner on your digital workstream is, and whether they can decide without a committee.
- Read the team's definition of done and check it includes testing and deployment.
- Attend the next sprint review and invite the customer's business contact.
- Put the sprint review dates on your project as milestones so the status report shows them.
- If retrospectives have stopped, offer to facilitate one 45-minute session.
