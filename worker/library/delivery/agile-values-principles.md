The Manifesto for Agile Software Development (2001) is short: four value statements and twelve principles. It was written by software developers, but the ideas carry over to any work where requirements are uncertain and fast feedback is possible, such as a customer portal, a self-serve ordering flow or an automation script for provisioning. This page restates them in Compass's words and groups the principles by theme.

## The four value pairs

Each pair names something the authors preferred, with the item on the right still valued, just less.

- **Individuals and interactions over processes and tools.** Talking to the right person usually solves a problem faster than another form or ticket queue.
- **Working software over comprehensive documentation.** A demo of a working feature tells the customer more than a long specification. In telecom terms, read it as "a working service": a circuit that passes traffic beats a perfect design document.
- **Customer collaboration over contract negotiation.** Work with the customer to find the right result, rather than arguing over the wording of the statement of work.
- **Responding to change over following a plan.** Plans are useful, but when the facts change, the plan should change with them.

Note what the pairs do not say: they do not reject documentation, contracts, processes or plans. A B2B project still needs a signed statement of work and a change process; agile asks you not to hide behind them.

## The principles, grouped by theme

The twelve principles fall naturally into six themes.

### Delivering value early and often

- The customer's first need is something useful in hand soon, followed by a steady flow of more.
- Hand over something that works every few weeks; the shorter the cycle, the sooner you can correct course.
- Measure progress by what actually works, not by documents produced or hours spent.

### Welcoming change

- A late change in what the customer needs is a chance to give them something more useful, not an intrusion to resist.

### People and collaboration

- The people who know the business and the people building the solution stay in daily contact, not only at hand-off points.
- Put motivated people at the centre, clear what blocks them and let them do the job without micromanagement.
- When information has to move inside a team, talking directly beats documents and long email chains.

### Sustainable pace

- Plan for a rhythm people can keep up for this project and the next; heroics are a warning sign, not a plan.

### Technical excellence and simplicity

- Well-built work is cheaper to change; corners cut now slow down every later change.
- Keep things simple: the work you decide not to do is often the biggest saving.

### Self-organisation and reflection

- Teams that decide how to organise their own work usually produce better designs than teams handed every detail.
- On a fixed rhythm, stop to look at how the team works and change one or two things.

## Where agile fits in B2B telecom

Agile thinking works best where the result can be built, shown and changed in small slices: portals, reporting dashboards, ordering integrations, automation. It fits less well where each step is physical and hard to undo: trenching fibre, installing equipment in a customer's data centre, or a cutover inside a fixed maintenance window (fenêtre de maintenance). Most real projects mix both, which is why hybrid delivery is common. Even on predictive work, several principles still apply directly: short feedback loops with the customer, measuring progress by working services, and regular retrospectives.

## Common misreadings

- "Agile means no plan." It means a plan that is expected to change.
- "Agile means no documentation." It means documentation that someone will use.
- "Agile means faster." It means earlier feedback, which often makes the total faster, but not every sprint.

## In practice

- Pick the next deliverable on your project and ask what the smallest version the customer could try would be.
- Book a 30-minute review with the customer's day-to-day contact to show progress, not slides.
- Hold a short retrospective (rétrospective) with your core team: keep, stop, try.
- Check whether anyone on the team is relying on heroics, and rebalance the work.
- Remove one report or form nobody reads.
